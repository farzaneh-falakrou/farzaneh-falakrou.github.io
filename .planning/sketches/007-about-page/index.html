I'm not sure when I became a designer, only that I never stopped being an architect. The first thing I drew with intention was a section through a building I couldn't have lived in if you'd paid me — and I've been drawing sections through things ever since. Through products, mostly.

— On the work

I think in plans, sections, and elevations.

Designing software felt like a natural extension of designing buildings. Both are about shaping how people move through a space. Both reward thinking in systems. And both fail in the same way — when surface beauty hides a structure that doesn't hold.

My work tends toward subtraction. I'd rather remove three features than add one. I'd rather have one button that's exactly right than three that are mostly fine. I write a lot of the copy myself, often before I open Figma — because if the words don't work, the layout can't save them.

— On the approach

Slow design, mostly.

I spend more time on the brief than on the artwork. Most of the projects I'm proud of started with a meeting where I argued against the thing we'd been asked to make. The output is usually smaller than what was requested, and usually does more.

I believe in shipping, but I'm allergic to "good enough." If a thing can be ten percent better with a day of additional attention, I'll take the day. The compound effect of those days is, I think, the only thing that separates one designer from another.

Chapter 01 — Where it started

Tehran, 2012.

I went into architecture because I wanted to design things people lived inside. Five years of school, three years at a residential studio in Tehran. The work was slow and rewarding in the way slow work usually is.

But I kept finding myself drawn to the digital tools we used to design buildings — and to the question of why those tools were so bad at it.

a photo from architecture school
Chapter 02 — The pivot

So I started making software.

In 2021 I joined Homelon as a designer. I expected it to feel like starting over. What I found instead was that the discipline of architecture — thinking in plans, sections, elevations; sweating circulation; reading drawings — translated almost directly.

The vocabulary was different. The rigor wasn't.
