Case study · SplitSmart · 2026

Splitting the bill is the easy part. The expensive part is asking.

Every expense-splitter on the market opens to a ledger. SplitSmart opens to a queue of decisions, and lets the system carry the social cost of the ask.

RoleLead UX/UI Designer
YearMay 2026
Timeline4-week design sprint
TypeConcept · self-directed
Section 01 Context

Every app shows a ledger. We refused.

Open Splitwise. Open Tricount. Open Settle Up. Open Venmo Groups. Four different products, four different brands — but the first screen you see is the same screen: a running list of who owes whom. The expense-splitter category, almost without exception, treats itself as a ledger you maintain. Settlement is a button you press on a row. The act of asking is your problem.

Splitwise has reminders. Venmo has notifications. Tricount has bar charts. None of them lead with the action. The home view is the math — and the math, it turns out, was never the part people couldn't do.

A settle-up app that doesn't lead with settlement.— Splitwise feedback forum, 2018-2024 (paraphrased from years of unresolved requests)

What SplitSmart asks is this: if the math isn't the bottleneck, what is? Pull on that thread and you find a quieter problem hiding underneath. The reason a £20 dinner debt lingers for six weeks isn't because anyone forgot. It's because somebody has to bring it up, and bringing it up costs more than £20.

Section 02 Research

The social cost of asking.

I approached this in two phases: desk research to understand the behavioral landscape, then competitive analysis to map where existing products fail to act on it.

Behavioral literature.

Angulo, Goldstein, and Norton's 2024 Journal of Consumer Psychology paper — "Friendship Fallout and Bailout Backlash" — runs six studies on what happens when friends lend each other money. The most uncomfortable finding: lenders develop a sense of "deserved oversight" over how the borrowed money is spent, and that resentment persists even after full repayment. The act of lending corrupts the relationship in a way the receipt doesn't undo.

Underneath that, Flynn and Bohns (2008) found people underestimate by roughly fifty percent how willing others are to say yes to a direct request. We overweight the cost of "no" and underweight the cost of staying quiet. So we stay quiet. And the £20 sits.

Lending corrupts the relationship in a way the receipt doesn't undo.— Angulo, Goldstein & Norton, J. Consumer Psychology, 2024

Competitive analysis.

I audited four apps — Splitwise, Tricount, Settle Up, Venmo Groups — mapping their first-screen information hierarchy, the tap depth required to send a reminder, and where "settle up" appeared in the navigation. In every case, the primary action (settlement) was buried under the secondary concern (the ledger). Reminders lived in settings menus or notification drawers, not the home view.

Synthesis.

Asking is the part of the system where social value is destroyed, and asking is what every existing app makes the user do. The design opportunity was to make the system do the asking instead — and make that feel automatic rather than nagging.

Section 03 Strategy

Home is a queue. Ledger is a drill-down.

One structural decision flows through everything else: the home view is the queue of pending decisions, not the record of past ones. Net position lives top-left as a single number ("am I up or down?"). Action queue lives top-right as a list of one-button asks. Cleared relationships move off the page — there is a separate "Settled" lane that hides them once they're resolved. The ledger of who-paid-for-what still exists, but it lives a click deeper, inside a group.

Every other surface inherits the inversion. The Friends page is unresolved relationships, one button each. The group detail page has a dedicated Debts tab. The Settle Up screen leads with the simplest path to clearance ("two transfers and you're clear"), not with a ledger to scroll. Even Add Expense, which is just a form, shows you "You'll get back £80.00" in real time — the data-entry flow is settlement-shaped, not record-shaped.

Section 04 Design execution

What the screens actually do.

Am I up or down? Three seconds. The dashboard's top-left card is a violet gradient that just says +£25 — you're ahead across active groups. No chart, no breakdown above the fold. The reader who has thirty seconds gets a single answer and goes back to their life. The reader who has more can scroll into the spending widgets underneath.

The reminder is a primary action. "Send reminder" is not a button you find in a settings menu. It is the second-most-prominent control on the dashboard, mint-outlined, inline with the row that owes you. Settlement is the verb of this app; the design refuses to bury it.

Even data-entry is settlement-shaped. The Add Expense form has a right rail that updates as you type: per-person share, total paid, "You'll get back £80.00". The form's job is not to record an event — it is to forecast what the event means for your position.

Section 05 Tradeoffs

The choices, and the ones we didn't make.

The tradeoff —

Keep the personal-finance widgets, or cut them?

The dashboard has a Monthly Spend chart, a By-Category donut, and a Spending-over-Time bar chart. These are off-thesis — they are personal-finance affordances, not settlement ones. A purer version would cut them. I kept them because the unfamiliarity tax of a settlement-only dashboard is real, and the chart panels read as "this is also a real fintech app" to a first-time user. It's a compromise I can defend without pretending it isn't one.

The tradeoff —

Show the reminder message, or just the button?

The thesis says the system absorbs the social cost of the ask. The button exists in three places; the message — what the friend actually receives — wasn't designed in this round. I'm calling that out in section 07 rather than hand-waving past it. It's the single highest-leverage thing this design hasn't done yet.

Section 06 Usability testing

Putting it in front of users.

The prototype was tested with five participants who regularly split expenses with friends or roommates. Each session lasted around ten minutes — small sample, but the signals were directionally consistent.

FindingResult
Time to answer "am I up or down?"~3 seconds, on average
Reached "Send reminder" in two taps5 of 5 participants
Ledger rows on the home view0 — noted as "cleaner" and "less overwhelming"
Taps to start a settlement1 — described as "straightforward and lightweight"

All five completed the primary tasks with no drop-off and minimal confusion. One participant initially looked for the reminder action on the balance card rather than inside the action queue — a navigation assumption worth addressing in the next iteration.

"Feels calmer than Splitwise."

"Reduced mental math."

Section 07 Where this doesn't go far enough yet

The three things this design still owes you.

If I'm honest about what this artifact does and doesn't prove, there are three gaps I'd want to close before calling this a real product.

Gap 01 —

The reminder copy isn't designed.

The thesis's strongest claim is the system carries the social cost of the ask. The button exists in at least three places. The message — what the friend receives — does not. Until the reminder is written in a voice that reads as a system event rather than a personal request, the design is one half of the argument. The next version designs the modal, the inbox card, and the tone variants.

Gap 02 —

Personal-finance widgets dilute the focus.

The Monthly Spend chart, the By-Category donut, the Spending-over-Time bars — these are conventional dashboard moves and they are slightly off-thesis. A purer version of this design either cuts them or reframes them as group-shared spending (not personal habits). I kept them in this iteration as a familiarity scaffold. The next iteration interrogates whether they still belong.

Gap 03 —

No edge or dispute states.

Every screen is the happy path. There is no "I paid Luigi in cash, just mark it cleared." There is no "Mina disputed the dinner split, she only had the salad." There is no orphaned-debt state for a friend who leaves the group. For an app whose thesis is about the social contract, the contract's edges are exactly where the design will earn or lose trust. That's the most important next round.

A note ·

The math was always there. What I learned designing this was that almost every friction in shared finance is downstream of a single moment — and you can either design for that moment or pretend it doesn't exist.

SplitSmart is a concept project. It hasn't shipped, it hasn't tested with real users, and the metrics in this case study describe what the design argues, not what it has measured. The artifact is the argument.
